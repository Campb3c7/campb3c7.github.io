Exam Objectives 18 testable items
  • ▶ Antimicrobial Use Strategy concept
    • ▶ Treatment intent types
      • Prophylaxis — prevent infection (pre-op, communicable disease, special populations)
      • Empiric — before culture results → use broad spectrum
      • Targeted/definitive — after cultures → narrow spectrum
    • ▶ Broad vs narrow spectrum
      • Broad: covers multiple organism groups or resistant organisms (MRSA, Pseudomonas)
      • Narrow: limited pathogen coverage — use after cultures or for mild infection
      • Escalation: broaden if no improvement 48–72h or clinical downturn
      • De-escalation: narrow once culture/susceptibility known
    • ▶ Stewardship — how prescribers perform it concept
      • Only prescribe when truly needed (not viral illness, CHF, asymptomatic bacteriuria)
      • Narrow ASAP when cultures return
      • Convert IV → PO when stable + high bioavailability agent available
      • Shorter courses preferred; rarely >7 days needed
  • ▶ Pharmacology Concepts concept
    • ▶ Bioavailability both
      • Definition: % absorbed orally (100% = IV = PO equivalent)
      • High bioavailability agents → PO preferred when stable
      • Memo: Fluoroquinolones, Doxycycline, Metronidazole, Linezolid, Clindamycin, Fluconazole, TMP-SMX
    • ▶ Why beta-lactams are preferred concept
      • Rapid bactericidal (cell wall rupture) → potent
      • Generally safe, well-tolerated, well-studied
      • Widely distributed; variety of IV and PO forms
    • ▶ Patient factors for dosing both
      • Renal function (CrCl via Cockcroft-Gault) → most antibiotics need adjustment
      • Elderly: muscle wasting → low CrCl even with normal Cr; higher adverse event risk
      • Weight/BMI: relevant for Vancomycin (weight-based dosing)
      • Severity of illness, comorbidities, allergy history
  • ▶ Beta-Lactam Allergy both
    • Only 10–15% of reported PCN allergies are true IgE-mediated
    • ▶ Investigate reported allergy
      • Intolerance/side effect (nausea) → any beta-lactam OK
      • Rash/hives → avoid agents with similar side chains (use chart reference)
      • Anaphylaxis → weigh risk/benefit; may need to avoid all beta-lactams
    • Cross-reactivity: due to side chain similarity, NOT beta-lactam core
    • Reference type: chart showing agents to avoid vs safe to use
  • ▶ Beta-Lactamase Inhibitors concept
    • Bacteria produce beta-lactamases → degrade beta-lactams → resistance
    • BLIs block these enzymes → restore/expand spectrum
    • ▶ Key combos to know memo
      • Amoxicillin/Clavulanate (Augmentin) — adds MSSA + anaerobes to aminopenicillin
      • Ampicillin/Sulbactam (Unasyn) — IV version of above
      • Piperacillin/Tazobactam (Zosyn) — broadest: adds Pseudomonas + anaerobes
  • ▶ Antibiotic Classes — Spectrum & DOC memo
    • ▶ Which class has broadest spectrum
      • Carbapenems — broadest of all antibiotic classes
      • Cover gram+, gram−, anaerobes, ESBL producers; NO MRSA
      • Ertapenem exception: no Pseudomonas, no Enterococcus
    • ▶ Drug of choice designations memo
      • MSSA → anti-staph penicillin (nafcillin) or 1st gen cephalosporin (cefazolin)
      • MRSA → Vancomycin (IV); TMP-SMX (PO)
      • Enterococcus faecalis → Aminopenicillin (ampicillin)
      • Strep pneumoniae → Ceftriaxone (3rd gen ceph)
      • Syphilis → Penicillin G
      • Bite wounds → Amox/Clav
      • ESBL gram-negatives → Carbapenems
      • Uncomplicated cystitis → Nitrofurantoin
      • C. diff (initial/recurrent) → Fidaxomicin or PO Vancomycin
    • ▶ Black Box Warnings memo
      • Fluoroquinolones: tendon rupture, myasthenia gravis exacerbation, aortic aneurysm/dissection, CNS effects, QT prolongation
      • Clindamycin: high risk C. diff (BBW)
      • Linezolid: bone marrow suppression (>14 days); serotonin syndrome risk with psych meds
  • ▶ Antivirals both
    • ▶ HSV antivirals memo
      • Acyclovir / Valacyclovir — cover HSV1, HSV2, VZV
      • HSV encephalitis → IV Acyclovir (drug of choice)
    • ▶ CMV — which patients at risk + treatment both
      • At risk: immunocompromised — HIV, transplant, oncology
      • Treatment: Ganciclovir (IV) / Valganciclovir (PO)
      • NOT first line for HSV/Zoster (higher toxicity)
    • ▶ Respiratory viruses / Influenza both
      • Agents: Oseltamivir (1st line), Zanamivir (inhaled), Baloxavir (single dose)
      • Who gets antivirals: hospitalized, severe/progressive illness, high-risk (age ≥65 or ≤2, immunocompromised, comorbidities)
      • Healthy outpatients: benefit is minimal (~1 day shorter illness)
      • Start within 48h of symptom onset ideally
    • ▶ COVID — steroids + outpatient tx both
      • Steroids: hospitalized patients requiring O2 or vent support ONLY (not outpatients)
      • Outpatient Rx: Paxlovid (preferred) — start within 5 days; many drug interactions
      • Alternative: Remdesivir IV x3 days (start within 7 days)
      • Who qualifies: ≥65, immunocompromised, multiple comorbidities, at risk for severe disease
  • ▶ Antifungals both
    • ▶ Why topical preferred for local infections concept
      • Low systemic exposure → minimal adverse events and drug interactions
      • Local candidiasis and tinea respond well to topical
    • ▶ Azoles — narrow vs broad spectrum memo
      • Fluconazole → narrowest: susceptible Candida only (C. albicans, C. parapsilosis)
      • Itraconazole → + endemic fungi (DOC Histoplasmosis)
      • Voriconazole → + Aspergillus, resistant Candida (DOC Aspergillosis); NO Mucorales
      • Posaconazole / Isavuconazole → broadest: + Mucorales
    • ▶ Terbinafine — unique indication memo
      • Oral: ONLY for onychomycosis (nail fungus)
      • Duration: fingernails 6+ weeks, toenails 12+ weeks
    • ▶ Echinocandins vs Azoles both
      • IV only (vs azoles which have PO options)
      • Better safety profile — fewer drug interactions, less hepatotoxicity
      • Cover Candida (including resistant strains) + Aspergillus
      • Do NOT cover Mucorales (unlike broadest azoles)
    • ▶ Empiric treatment of fungemia / invasive candidiasis memo
      • Use Echinocandin — broader coverage than Fluconazole, better safety
      • Especially in critically ill and neutropenic patients

Slides — All Content 156 slides · 6 major sections
  • ▶ A. Antibiotic Use Overview concept
    • ▶ Definitions & strategies
      • Prophylaxis (pre-op, communicable disease prevention, special populations)
      • Empiric coverage — broad, before cultures
      • Targeted therapy — narrow, after cultures
      • De-escalation and escalation — stepwise based on cultures + patient status
      • Antibiogram — local resistance patterns guide empiric choice
    • ▶ Pharmacology / PK concepts
      • Bioavailability — definition, high-bioavailability drug list
      • IV vs PO — when each is required (endocarditis, osteomyelitis, meningitis = usually IV)
      • Distribution — BBB crossing, tissue-specific concentration (FQ→bone, nitro→urine)
      • Renal dysfunction — CrCl adjustment, Cockcroft-Gault, reduced efficacy vs toxicity
      • Elderly — muscle wasting, low CrCl, highest prescribing rate
      • Cost — PO cheaper than IV; cost ≠ spectrum quality
    • ▶ How to manage antimicrobial therapy (5-step framework)
      • 1. Determine infection (site → organisms → cultures)
      • 2. Patient factors (severity, allergies, renal function, comorbidities)
      • 3. Design initial regimen (reach site, cover organisms, use PO if possible)
      • 4. Modify daily (narrow on cultures, stop unnecessary agents, IV→PO)
      • 5. Duration + monitoring (shorter preferred, labs for courses >2 weeks)
  • ▶ B. Microbiology Review — Common Pathogens context only
    • ▶ Gram-positive cocci
      • Strep: viridans group (alpha-hemolytic), Group A/B/C/G (beta-hemolytic), Strep pneumo (diplococci), Enterococcus
      • Group A Strep → pharyngitis, cellulitis, necrotizing fasciitis
      • Group B Strep → neonatal sepsis, UTI, DFI (diabetics)
      • Strep pneumo → #1 CAP + bacterial meningitis
      • Enterococcus → UTI, intra-abdominal, bacteremia; E. faecium more resistant (VRE)
      • Staph aureus — MSSA vs MRSA; coagulase positive; highly pathogenic
      • CoNS (Staph epidermidis) — skin flora, mostly contaminants; Staph lugdunensis exception (virulent)
      • Staph saprophyticus → UTI (young women)
    • ▶ Gram-negative bacilli
      • Enterics (Enterobacterales): E. coli (#1 UTI), Klebsiella, Serratia, Enterobacter, Proteus — may produce ESBLs
      • Non-enterics: H. influenzae, Moraxella (CAP); Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter (nosocomial MDROs)
    • ▶ Anaerobes
      • Gram-neg: Bacteroides fragilis (most resistant, colon dominant)
      • Almost always polymicrobial; associated with necrotic/foul-smell infections
      • "Covers anaerobes" = covers B. fragilis specifically
    • ▶ Atypicals
      • Legionella, Mycoplasma, Chlamydia pneumoniae — intracellular, not Gram-stained
      • Detected by PCR or Legionella urinary antigen
    • ▶ Pathogens by infection type
      • UTI: E. coli ~90% (uncomplicated); gram-positives possible
      • Cellulitis: Strep (non-purulent); Staph aureus (purulent/abscess)
      • DFI: polymicrobial (gram+, gram−, anaerobes); Pseudomonas with water exposure
      • CAP: Strep pneumo, H. influenzae, Moraxella, atypicals, viruses
      • Nosocomial PNA: Pseudomonas, E. coli, Klebsiella, MRSA
      • Intra-abdominal: Strep species, enteric gram-negs, anaerobes; Enterococcus in gallbladder
  • ▶ C. Antimicrobial Stewardship concept
    • Purpose: optimize use → better outcomes, less resistance, lower cost, less C. diff
    • ~30% of hospital antibiotic prescribing is unnecessary/suboptimal
    • ▶ Dangers of antibiotic use
      • Adverse events (~20% of hospitalized patients receiving antibiotics)
      • C. diff — 85% had antibiotics in prior 28 days; 223,900 cases/year; $6.3B cost
      • Resistance — 2.8M resistant infections/year in US; 35,000 deaths
    • When NOT to prescribe: viral illness, CHF, dementia/dehydration, asymptomatic bacteriuria, positive flu test in healthy patient
  • ▶ D. Antibiotic Classes memo-heavy
    • ▶ Beta-Lactams — mechanism + allergy
      • Mechanism: cell wall rupture → bactericidal; broadly distributed
      • Allergy: only 10–15% true IgE; cross-reactivity via side chain (not core ring)
      • 3 tiers: intolerance → rash → anaphylaxis (different management for each)
    • ▶ Penicillins (4 subgroups)
      • Penicillin → narrow; DOC syphilis, dental, targeted Strep
      • Anti-staph (nafcillin, oxacillin, dicloxacillin) → DOC MSSA
      • Aminopenicillins (amox, ampicillin) → DOC E. faecalis, beta-hemolytic Strep
      • Amox/clav, amp/sulbactam → + MSSA, anaerobes, more gram-neg; DOC bite wounds
      • Pip/tazo (Zosyn) → broadest penicillin: adds Pseudomonas
    • ▶ Cephalosporins (generations 1–4)
      • 1st gen (cefazolin, cephalexin) → DOC MSSA; surgical prophylaxis
      • 2nd gen (cefuroxime, cefoxitin) → + Strep pneumo, improved gram-neg; cefoxitin + anaerobes
      • 3rd gen (ceftriaxone) → DOC Strep pneumo, gonorrhea; ceftazidime = Pseudomonas
      • 4th gen (cefepime) → broad: gram+, gram−, Pseudomonas; no anaerobes
      • None cover Enterococcus
    • ▶ Carbapenems
      • Broadest class overall (gram+, gram−, anaerobes, ESBL)
      • NO MRSA coverage; Ertapenem also lacks Pseudomonas + Enterococcus
      • DOC ESBL-producing gram-negatives
      • Reserve for nosocomial/resistant infections
    • ▶ Fluoroquinolones
      • Mechanism: stop DNA replication; high bioavailability
      • BBW: tendon rupture, myasthenia exacerbation, aortic aneurysm, CNS effects, QT prolongation
      • Cipro → UTI, Pseudomonas; NO reliable Strep pneumo
      • Levofloxacin → CAP + Pseudomonas coverage
      • Moxifloxacin → CAP + anaerobes; NO Pseudomonas; NO UTI
    • ▶ Macrolides
      • Azithromycin — most tolerated; CAP (combo due to high Strep pneumo resistance ~45%), URTI, Chlamydia (2nd line)
      • Anti-inflammatory property = added benefit in COPD
    • ▶ Tetracyclines
      • Doxycycline — SSTI (incl. MRSA), COPD, CAP, Chlamydia (1st line)
      • NOT for UTI (GI elimination, not concentrated in urine)
    • ▶ Other antibiotics (Vanco, Linezolid, TMP-SMX, Clinda, Metro, Nitro, Fidaxo)
      • Vancomycin — IV: DOC MRSA; gram-positive broad; nephrotoxic; weight-based dosing; PO ONLY for C. diff
      • Linezolid — gram-positive broad incl. VRE; excellent bioavailability; bone marrow suppression >14d; drug interactions with psych meds
      • TMP-SMX — DOC MRSA (PO), DOC UTI; immunocompromised prophylaxis; folic acid synthesis inhibitor
      • Clindamycin — SSTI, dental; BBW C. diff; NOT first line (high resistance)
      • Metronidazole — anaerobes + protozoa only; Trichomonas, C. diff combo, anaerobic coverage
      • Nitrofurantoin — uncomplicated cystitis ONLY (high urine, low serum); avoid CrCl <30
      • Fidaxomicin — C. diff first line; macrolide, not absorbed GI; expensive
  • ▶ E. Antivirals both
    • ▶ Herpesviruses
      • HSV1 (cold sores), HSV2 (genital), VZV (chickenpox/shingles), CMV
      • Retained for life; immunocompromised at risk for reactivation → suppressive therapy
      • Acyclovir/Valacyclovir → HSV1, HSV2, VZV; IV Acyclovir = DOC HSV encephalitis
      • Ganciclovir/Valganciclovir → CMV (HIV, transplant, oncology); bone marrow suppression risk
    • ▶ Influenza
      • Oseltamivir (Tamiflu) — PO, preferred for hospitalized/pregnant/immunocompromised; 5-day course
      • Zanamivir — inhaled; Baloxavir — single dose, newer
      • Treat if: hospitalized, severe, high-risk; NOT routine healthy outpatient
    • ▶ COVID-19
      • Mild → home; Moderate → dyspnea/hospitalized; Severe → hypoxia (O2 ≤94%) or vent
      • Outpatient: Paxlovid (preferred, within 5d); Remdesivir IV x3d (within 7d)
      • Steroids: hospitalized requiring O2 or vent ONLY
      • Who qualifies for outpatient Rx: ≥65, immunocompromised, multiple comorbidities
  • ▶ F. Antifungals both
    • ▶ Fungal infection overview
      • Immunocompetent → local disease only (thrush, tinea, vaginal candidiasis)
      • Immunocompromised (leukemia, transplant, HIV, burns) → invasive/systemic disease
      • Risk factors: prolonged antibiotics, high colonization, prior fungal infection
    • ▶ Fungal types
      • Yeasts: Candida (most common; C. albicans), Cryptococcus, PCP
      • Molds: Aspergillus (COPD/asthma risk), Mucorales (rare, severe)
      • Endemic: Histoplasma (Midwest/Ohio river valley), Blastomyces (Eastern/Central US), Coccidioides (Southwest)
    • ▶ Topical antifungals
      • Nystatin → Candida ONLY (not tinea)
      • Azole creams (miconazole, clotrimazole, etc.) → Candida + Tinea
    • ▶ Terbinafine
      • Oral: onychomycosis ONLY; fingernails 6wk+, toenails 12wk+
      • Topical: OTC, tinea infections only (NOT nails)
    • ▶ Systemic Azoles (spectrum ladder)
      • Fluconazole → narrowest; susceptible Candida; good BBB penetration; good urinary concentration; DOC candidemia (susceptible)
      • Itraconazole → + endemic fungi; DOC Histoplasmosis; variable GI absorption limits use
      • Voriconazole → + Aspergillus + resistant Candida (C. krusei, C. glabrata); DOC Aspergillosis; NO Mucorales
      • Posaconazole/Isavuconazole → broadest; + Mucorales; reserved for severe immunocompromised
      • All azoles: hepatotoxicity risk (monitor LFTs weekly); drug interactions
    • ▶ Echinocandins (Caspofungin, Micafungin, Anidulafungin)
      • IV only; cover Candida (all strains incl. resistant) + Aspergillus
      • Better safety than azoles; fewer drug interactions
      • DOC: empiric invasive candidiasis, critically ill / neutropenic patients
      • Do NOT cover Mucorales
